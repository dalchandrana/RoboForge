# Differential Drive Kinematics: How Two-Wheel Robots Turn

Almost every mobile robot uses **differential-drive steering**. 

Cars use complex steering racks to turn their front wheels. In contrast, a differential robot has two fixed drive wheels. A passive caster ball balances the chassis.

We steer by varying the individual speeds of the left and right motors!

---

## 1. The Anatomy of a 2WD Mobile Robot

Consider our standard RoboForge 2WD chassis (matching Starter Kit and Project P3/P4):

- **Wheel Radius ($R$):** The distance from axle to rubber tread. Standard yellow TT motors use $66\,\text{mm}$ wheels ($R = 3.3\,\text{cm}$).
- **Wheelbase ($L$):** The distance between the left and right wheel contact points (typically $L = 13.5\,\text{cm}$).
- **Caster Wheel:** An omnidirectional ball that supports the chassis without steering.

```
       [ Front Caster Ball ]
                |
          +-----------+
[Left] ===|  Chassis  |===[Right]
Wheel     |  Body     |   Wheel
(omega_L) +-----------+   (omega_R)
                |
          <- Wheelbase L ->
```

---

## 2. Forward and Angular Kinematics

Each wheel moves forward with linear speed:

$$v_L = R \times \omega_L \quad \text{and} \quad v_R = R \times \omega_R$$

The forward speed $v$ of the robot center is the average of both wheels:

$$v = \frac{v_R + v_L}{2} = \frac{R}{2} (\omega_R + \omega_L)$$

The turning rate $\omega$ depends on the wheel speed difference divided by wheelbase $L$:

$$\omega = \frac{v_R - v_L}{L} = \frac{R}{L} (\omega_R - \omega_L)$$

---

## 3. Four Core Maneuvers

By controlling motor directions and PWM duty cycles, we command four motions:

### 1. Straight Line Forward ($\omega_L = \omega_R > 0$)
- Linear velocity: $v = R \times \omega$
- Angular velocity: $\omega = 0$
- The robot travels straight forward.

### 2. In-Place Zero-Radius Spin ($\omega_L = -\omega_R$)
- Linear velocity: $v = 0$
- Angular velocity: $\omega = \frac{2 R}{L} \omega_R$
- The robot spins in place around its own center. This lets mobile robots escape tight dead-ends!

### 3. Pivot Turn ($\omega_L = 0, \omega_R > 0$)
- One wheel locks stationary.
- The other wheel drives forward, swinging the robot around the locked wheel.

### 4. Smooth Curved Arc ($\omega_R > \omega_L > 0$)
- Both wheels move forward, but at different speeds.
- The robot follows a smooth circular curve around an **Instantaneous Center of Curvature (ICC)**.

<Callout type="safety">
**Tabletop and Mechanical Safety Directives:**
1. **Never test driving on an elevated table:** A bug can drive the robot over the edge in seconds. This will break the chassis. Always test on the floor.
2. **Pinch hazard:** Gearmotors generate strong torque. Keep fingers and loose hair away from spinning wheel rims.
3. **Low-voltage limit:** Power motors from a battery pack ($\le 6\,\text{V}$) with current $\le 2\,\text{A}$.
</Callout>

## 4. Summary & Practical Insights

In code, the Arduino microcontroller controls wheel speeds using PWM:

```cpp
// Tank steering function for L298N driver
void setMotorSpeeds(int leftPwm, int rightPwm) {
  // Positive PWM = forward, negative PWM = reverse
  setLeftMotor(leftPwm);
  setRightMotor(rightPwm);
}

// Spin 90 degrees right in place
void spinRightInPlace() {
  setMotorSpeeds(180, -180); // Left forward, Right backward
}
```
