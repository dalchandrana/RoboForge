## 1. Hook
Have you ever wondered why a 9V rectangular battery cannot shock you when you touch its terminals with your fingers, but can instantly illuminate an LED or spin a tiny motor? 

The secret lies in the difference between the **electric push** (voltage) and the **charge flow rate** (current).

## 2. Voltage: The Electric Pressure
Voltage (symbol $V$) is measured in **Volts**. Think of voltage as electric pressure created by chemical reactions inside a battery. 

A battery has two terminals:
- **Positive terminal (+)**: High electric potential.
- **Negative terminal (-)**: Low electric potential.

The voltage rating (like $1.5\text{ V}$ for an AA battery or $9\text{ V}$ for a rectangular transistor battery) measures the potential difference between these two poles. The higher the voltage, the harder charges are pushed!

<Callout type="safety" title="Low-Voltage Laboratory Boundary">
In our school robotics curriculum, all battery experiments use safe low-voltage DC power (1.5V to 12V). Human skin has high dry resistance, making voltages under 24V DC completely safe from electrical shock. Never connect anything to AC mains power.
</Callout>

## 3. Current: The Rate of Flow
Current (symbol $I$) is measured in **Amperes** (or **milliamps**, where $1\text{ A} = 1000\text{ mA}$). 

Current tells us how many Coulombs of charge pass through a wire cross-section every single second:
$$I = \frac{Q}{t}$$

In typical robotics circuits:
- A small LED draws about $15\text{ mA}$ to $20\text{ mA}$ ($0.02\text{ A}$).
- A micro servo motor draws about $100\text{ mA}$ to $500\text{ mA}$.
- A tiny digital sensor draws under $5\text{ mA}$.

## 4. Voltage Causes Current
Voltage does not "flow" through a wire — **voltage exists across** two points, and this potential difference causes **current to flow through** the component.

<Callout type="info" title="Think like an Engineer">
You measure **voltage across** a component (in parallel) and **current through** a wire (in series).
</Callout>

## 5. Summary Check
Remember:
1. Voltage is the electrical push ($V$, in Volts).
2. Current is the rate of charge flow ($I$, in Amperes).
3. Current only flows when there is both an electric push (voltage) AND a complete closed circuit path.
