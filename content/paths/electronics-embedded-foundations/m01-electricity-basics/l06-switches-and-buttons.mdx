## 1. Hook: Taking Control
So far, circuits operate continuously the moment a battery is connected. But what if you want to turn on a robot headlight only in the dark, or start a motor when a bumper strikes a wall?

Switches provide human and mechanical control over electrical current.

## 2. Opening and Closing the Loop
A switch is simply a mechanical gap controller:
- **Open (OFF):** Metal contacts are physically separated. Air between the contacts has billions of Ohms of resistance, so current stops completely ($I = 0$).
- **Closed (ON):** Metal contacts touch firmly, creating a continuous low-resistance bridge (under $0.05\,\Omega$). Current flows freely.

## 3. Momentary vs Latching
- **Latching Switch:** Stays in its new position after you flip it (like your household light switch or breadboard toggle rocker).
- **Momentary Pushbutton:** Returns to its default state as soon as you release your finger:
  - **Normally Open (NO):** Disconnected by default; pressing closes the connection (like a doorbell or keyboard key).
  - **Normally Closed (NC):** Connected by default; pressing breaks the connection (like an emergency stop button).

<Callout type="safety" title="Emergency Stop Habit">
In mobile robotics and motorized projects, always install a master power switch or accessible battery pull-plug as an emergency stop. If a motor stalls or software crashes, cutting power instantly prevents burned motors and dead batteries.
</Callout>

## 4. Preventing Floating Pins
When connecting a button to a digital microcontroller input (like Arduino Pin 2):
- When the button is pressed, the pin connects firmly to $5\text{ V}$ (HIGH).
- When released, what is the pin connected to? **Nothing!** It is an open floating wire antenna that picks up stray electrostatic noise, causing erratic jumping between 0 and 1!

To solve this, we add a high-value resistor ($10\,\text{k}\Omega$) called a **pull-down resistor** to gently tie the pin to $0\text{ V}$ Ground whenever the button is unpressed.
